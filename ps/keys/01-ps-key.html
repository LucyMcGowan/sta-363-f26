Answer key, Problem set 1: Framing the problem

STA 363 | Instructor copy

1. When flexibility helps

The trade-off in every part is the same. A flexible method lowers bias because it can match a wider range of shapes for \(f\), and it raises variance because it has more freedom to chase whatever is specific to the training sample. Whether flexibility helps depends on which of those two terms dominates in the setting described.

(a) Better. With very large \(n\) and small \(p\) there is enough data to estimate a flexible \(f\) precisely, so the variance cost of flexibility is small. The flexible method keeps the lower bias without paying much for it.

(b) Worse. With \(p\) very large and \(n\) small there are too few observations to pin down a flexible fit, so its variance is large and it will fit the noise in the small sample. The more constrained method is more stable here.

(c) Better. If the true \(f\) is highly non-linear, an inflexible method such as linear regression has large bias that no amount of data removes, while a flexible method can bend to follow the true shape and lower that bias.

(d) Worse. A large \(\text{Var}(\varepsilon)\) means the response carries a lot of noise around \(f\). A flexible method has the freedom to fit that noise, which inflates its variance, while the signal it is trying to recover has not changed. Because \(\text{Var}(\varepsilon)\) is the irreducible error, no method can drive test error below it.

2. Parametric or not

(a) A parametric method first assumes a functional form for \(f\), for example the linear form \(f(X) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \dots + \beta_p X_p\), and then uses the training data to estimate the finite set of parameters that form requires, so estimating \(f\) reduces to estimating those parameters. A non-parametric method makes no explicit assumption about the form of \(f\). It estimates \(f\) directly by looking for a function that fits the observed data closely while staying smooth, and because it does not collapse the problem to a few parameters it needs far more observations for an accurate estimate.

(b) Advantage of the parametric approach: it reduces \(f\) to a small number of parameters, so it can be estimated well from a modest sample and the resulting model is easy to interpret. Disadvantage: the assumed form is usually not the true \(f\), and if it is a poor approximation the estimate stays biased no matter how much data is collected.

(c) Flexibility is bought by adding parameters and functional freedom, and every degree of freedom added makes the fitted \(f\) harder to describe in simple terms. A linear model gives one coefficient per predictor, each with a direct “hold the others fixed and \(Y\) changes by this much” reading, whereas a method flexible enough to capture strong non-linearities and interactions, such as a boosted tree ensemble, has no comparable short description. The two axes in Figure 2.7 point in opposite directions because they are two views of the same underlying model complexity rather than settings that can be dialed independently, so a method near the top on flexibility sits near the bottom on interpretability as a consequence.

3. Regression, classification, prediction, inference

(a) Regression, since CEO salary is quantitative. The goal is inference, since the aim is to understand which factors affect salary rather than to predict a new firm’s salary. Here \(n = 500\) firms and \(p = 3\) predictors (profit, number of employees, industry).

(b) Classification, since the response is success or failure. The goal is prediction, since the aim is to guess the outcome for a new product. Here \(n = 20\) products and \(p = 13\) predictors (price, marketing budget, competition price, plus the ten other variables).

(c) Regression, since a weekly percent change is quantitative. The goal is prediction of the USD/Euro change from the market changes. Here \(n = 52\) weeks in 2012 and \(p = 3\) predictors (the percent changes in the US, British, and German markets).

4. Classification and regression applications

Full credit requires, for each of four applications, a named response, at least two named predictors, a prediction-or-inference label, and one sentence of justification. The response has to be categorical for the two classification examples and quantitative for the two regression examples. The sample answers below are one acceptable set.

Classification, example 1: email spam filtering. Response: whether an incoming message is spam or not spam. Predictors: counts of particular words in the body, age of the sender’s domain, number of links, whether the sender is in the user’s address book. Goal: prediction, because the filter only has to route each new message correctly and no one needs an explanation of the rule.

Classification, example 2: loan default. Response: whether a borrower defaults or repays within the loan term. Predictors: credit score, debt-to-income ratio, length of employment, loan amount relative to income. Goal: inference, because a lender wants to know which applicant characteristics drive default risk in order to set lending policy.

Regression, example 1: house sale price. Response: sale price in dollars. Predictors: square footage, number of bedrooms, age of the house, distance to the city center. Goal: prediction, because a listing site wants an accurate price estimate for each new house that comes on the market.

Regression, example 2: crop yield. Response: yield in bushels per acre. Predictors: total rainfall over the season, pounds of fertilizer applied per acre, average growing-season temperature. Goal: inference, because an agronomist wants to estimate how much yield changes per additional unit of fertilizer.

5. MSE in training versus test sets

The sketch has flexibility on the x-axis and error on the y-axis, with three labeled curves.

Training error starts moderate at low flexibility and decreases monotonically as flexibility increases, approaching zero, because a flexible enough method can fit the training points almost exactly.

Test error is U-shaped: high at low flexibility because the fit is too rigid to capture \(f\) (bias), falling as flexibility increases, reaching a minimum at some intermediate flexibility, then rising again as the method starts to overfit (variance).

Irreducible error is a horizontal line at \(\text{Var}(\varepsilon)\) that does not depend on the method.

The sketch should also show that the test error curve stays above the irreducible error line everywhere, with its minimum close to but still above that line, that the training error curve drops below the irreducible error line at high flexibility, and that the training and test curves are close at low flexibility and separate as flexibility grows.

Data from James, G., Witten, D., Hastie, T., Tibshirani, R., and Taylor, J. (2023). An Introduction to Statistical Learning with Applications in Python. Springer.
