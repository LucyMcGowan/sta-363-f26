Application exercise 14: Regression trees

STA 363

A regression tree splits the predictor space into boxes and predicts the mean response in each one. The first three parts work with Hitters, predicting log salary from Years in the major leagues and Hits the previous season, the two predictors in ISLP Figure 8.1. Part 4 grows and prunes a tree on a second data set.

Part 2: Finding a split

Five observations of one predictor \(x\) and a response \(y\):

\(y\) 1 3 2 7 9

A split at cutpoint \(s\) sends every observation with \(x < s\) to the left region and the rest to the right, and predicts each region with its mean.

  1. On paper, fill in the table.
  2. What is the RSS before any split, when all five observations are predicted with their overall mean? Which cutpoint lowers it the most?
  3. After that split, what does the tree predict for a new observation with \(x = 4.2\)?

split_rss computes the last column of the table. Check your answers against it.

def split_rss(x, y, s):
    left, right = y[x < s], y[x >= s]
    return ((left - left.mean()) ** 2).sum() + ((right - right.mean()) ** 2).sum()


x_toy = np.array([1, 2, 3, 4, 5])
y_toy = np.array([1, 3, 2, 7, 9])
[float(split_rss(x_toy, y_toy, s)) for s in [1.5, 2.5, 3.5, 4.5]]
[32.75, 28.0, 4.0, 20.75]

On Hitters the search runs over every cutpoint between adjacent observed values of each predictor. best_split returns the winning cutpoint, the RSS after the split, and how many players land on each side.

def best_split(x, y):
    values = np.sort(x.unique())
    cuts = (values[:-1] + values[1:]) / 2
    rss = [split_rss(x, y, s) for s in cuts]
    s = cuts[int(np.argmin(rss))]
    return {"cutpoint": s, "RSS after": min(rss),
            "left": int((x < s).sum()), "right": int((x >= s).sum())}

Part 4: Pruning a tree on Boston

Boston has the median home value medv, in thousands of dollars, for 506 census tracts around Boston, along with 12 predictors. Among them, rm is the average number of rooms per home and lstat is the percentage of residents with lower socioeconomic status. ISLP Lab 8.3.2 fits a regression tree to the same data.

Boston = pd.read_csv(DATA + "Boston.csv")
X_b = Boston.drop(columns=["medv"])
y_b = Boston["medv"]

big_b = DecisionTreeRegressor(min_samples_leaf=5, random_state=363).fit(X_b, y_b)
path_b = big_b.cost_complexity_pruning_path(X_b, y_b)
search_b = GridSearchCV(
    DecisionTreeRegressor(min_samples_leaf=5, random_state=363),
    {"ccp_alpha": path_b.ccp_alphas},
    cv=cv, scoring="neg_mean_squared_error",
).fit(X_b, y_b)
pruned_b = search_b.best_estimator_

int(big_b.get_n_leaves()), int(pruned_b.get_n_leaves()), round(float(-search_b.best_score_), 2)
(82, 42, 22.21)

cv_table has one row per subtree on the pruning path, with its number of leaves, its CV MSE, and the standard error of that CV MSE across the ten folds.

fold_mse = -pd.DataFrame(search_b.cv_results_).filter(regex=r"^split\d+_test_score$")
cv_table = pd.DataFrame({
    "ccp_alpha": path_b.ccp_alphas,
    "leaves": [
        DecisionTreeRegressor(min_samples_leaf=5, random_state=363, ccp_alpha=a).fit(X_b, y_b).get_n_leaves()
        for a in path_b.ccp_alphas

    "training_mse": path_b.impurities,
    "cv_mse": fold_mse.mean(axis=1).to_numpy(),
    "cv_se": fold_mse.std(axis=1).to_numpy() / np.sqrt(10),
})
shown = cv_table[cv_table["leaves"] > 1]

The one standard error rule wants the smallest tree that is still competitive, so take the minimum number of leaves that clears the threshold.

A regression tree is grown greedily, one split at a time, and then pruned back by cost complexity, with \(\alpha\) chosen by cross-validation.
